Please don’t park

  • On Meadow Street or Mill Street, in any circumstances. There are complicated access arrangements there.
  • Across the entrance to the working farm.
  • Across access to the house at the far side of our car park.
  • Outside neighbours’ houses.

Please always park responsibly. It’s a lovely village, but parking can be a problem. Thank you for helping us stay good neighbours.

Two villages, one hall

Farms and moorland, a canal, a cotton mill and a quarrel over a church. How Heapey and Wheelton grew, and how a Victorian school became our village hall.

Heapey and Wheelton sit side by side on the edge of the West Pennine Moors, between Chorley and Blackburn. For centuries this was farming country. Then came the canal, the cotton mill and the schools that shaped the villages we know today. Our hall began life as one of those schools.

The Middle AgesFarms, moors and monks

Both villages are old. Heapey appears in records as “Hepay” by 1260, and a chapel there is first noted in the 1550s, the forerunner of today’s St Barnabas’ Church.

Much of Wheelton’s land passed to the monks of Whalley Abbey. When the abbey was dissolved by Henry VIII, the “manor of Wheelton” was sold in 1539–40 to Sir Richard Hoghton, of the family of nearby Hoghton Tower.

The 1800sWeavers, water and a canal

Before the big mills, cotton was woven at home. Handloom weavers settled in the valleys, where springs fed the streams they needed, and some of their cottages still stand. In 1835 a cotton mill opened at White Coppice, driven by a water wheel. In Heapey, bleach works were fed by a chain of reservoirs, which you can still walk around today.

The Leeds and Liverpool Canal arrived too. At Johnson’s Hillock, a flight of seven locks, built around 1816 as the final stretch of the canal, lifts boats about 66 feet up the hillside to the Top Lock.

1859 to the 1970sPeter Todd’s village

In 1859 the mill owner Peter Todd built Victoria Mill, known locally as Todd’s Mill. Its steam engine burned coal that came by barge from the Wigan coalfield, unloaded at the Top Lock. The mill grew the village fast: Wheelton’s population went from 1,041 in 1851 to 1,538 in 1891, and Todd and his successors built most of the village’s brick terraces between 1856 and 1880.

The mill dominated the skyline for a century. It closed in the 1960s and was demolished in the early 1970s. Houses now stand on the site, at Millbrook Close. That’s why the bus stop nearest the hall still carries the mill’s memory in its name.

Unable to settle their differences with the vicar, Peter Todd and his followers left the established church and set up their own.

1868 to 1983A school born of a quarrel

In 1868 Peter Todd fell out bitterly with the local vicar. Todd and his supporters broke away and founded their own Free Church of England congregation, with its own school. Their school-chapel, St Paul’s, was built in 1871 at the top of Meadow Street. That building is our village hall today.

In 1905 St Paul’s became Wheelton Council School, and generations of village children were taught there. When the school was no longer needed, a group of villagers formed a steering committee to find a home for a village hall. They bought the old school, won permission to change its use, and renovated it bit by bit. One of the first jobs was levelling the main hall floor, where sunken tiled areas had been used for washing in the school days.

The building has been our village hall since 1983. Today it is a registered charity (no. 515631), cared for by volunteer trustees, and used by nurseries, clubs, classes, parties and meetings.

1922The clock tower

At the centre of Wheelton stands the war memorial clock tower, shared by both villages and set in its own small garden. It was unveiled in August 1922 by the Earl of Crawford and Balcarres. Plaques on the tower record the names and regiments of the men of Heapey and Wheelton who died in both world wars. The tower is Grade II listed, and it appears in our village events logo.
